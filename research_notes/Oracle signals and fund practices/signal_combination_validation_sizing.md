# Signal Combination, Validation, Sizing and Timing for a Small Discretionary-plus-Systematic US Equity Process

Scope note: methods usable with free daily data and a universe of tens to a few hundred US stocks. Not financial advice. Sources tagged [Academic], [Practitioner] (fund-affiliated research, such as AQR or Man Group), or [Secondary] (summaries, vendor docs, study notes). A secondary source is used only where I could not reach the primary paper, and is flagged.

## Q1. What is the minimum validation standard a one-person system should hold a new signal to before trading it?

### Takeaway
A one-person system should log every variant it tests, then demand three things of a signal: a multiple-testing-aware hurdle (t-stat around 3, or a Deflated Sharpe Ratio of at least 0.95 that counts all trials), evidence that it holds up out of sample (an untouched holdout, or a CPCV/PBO estimate showing the in-sample winner does not usually rank below the median out of sample), and profitability net of costs on point-in-time, survivorship-free data. Size the position as if the signal will lose about a third to a half of its backtested edge, because published anomalies do.

### Cited Findings

**Multiple-testing hurdles**
- Harvey, Liu & Zhu argue that, given the hundreds of factors already tested, "a newly discovered factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a t-statistic greater than 3.0" rather than the conventional 2.0. They provide cutoffs over time from 1967 onward and argue that most claimed findings in financial economics are likely false. Published in Review of Financial Studies 29(1):5–68 (2016). [Academic] — [NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592); [SSRN 2513152](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2513152)
- Counterpoint on replicability: Chen & Zimmermann reproduce 319 published predictors. Of the 161 that were clearly significant in the original papers, about 98% of the reproduced long-short portfolios have t > 1.96. A regression of reproduced on original t-stats has a slope of 0.90 and an R² of 83%. Returns decay after publication but stay positive. Published in Critical Finance Review 11(2), 2022. The Fed hosts free monthly long-short returns for about 205 predictors. [Academic] — [Federal Reserve FEDS page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/open-source-cross-sectional-asset-pricing.htm); [SSRN 3604626](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3604626); [CFR](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/CFR-0112). There is also a 2022 arXiv paper titled "Most claimed statistical findings in cross-sectional return predictability are likely true" ([arXiv 2206.15365](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.15365)), which disputes Harvey, Liu & Zhu's pessimism. The hurdle debate is unresolved.
- Out-of-sample decay: McLean & Pontiff (Journal of Finance 71(1), 2016) study 97 predictors and find investors learn about mispricing from publications. Post-publication short interest and volume in the predictor portfolios rise, and correlations with other published predictors increase. They reject both "predictability disappears entirely" and "predictability is unchanged." On magnitude, the working paper reported about a 35% post-publication decay, and a secondary summary of the published version reports 58% (with a smaller decline in the out-of-sample but pre-publication period). The figures differ by version, so check the published abstract. [Academic] — [Gwern copy of published paper](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf); [Ivey working paper](https://ivey.uwo.ca/media/3775549/pontiff.pdf); [CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/big-ideas/effects-of-market-adaptation)

**Deflated Sharpe Ratio and PBO**
- Bailey & López de Prado's Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR) corrects a backtested Sharpe for two sources of inflation: selection bias under multiple testing (the number of trials) and non-normal returns (skew and kurtosis). It extends the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (López de Prado, 2012). Published in Journal of Portfolio Management 40(5):94–107 (2014). [Academic] — [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551). An implementation exists in R quantstrat as `deflatedSharpe` — [rdocumentation](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/quantstrat/versions/0.16.7/topics/deflatedSharpe)
- Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu define the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO) and estimate it with Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV). CSCV is model-free and nonparametric: split the time series into S blocks, form all train/test combinations, pick the best configuration in-sample, and record its out-of-sample rank. PBO is the share of combinations where that in-sample winner ranks below the out-of-sample median. The authors argue standard hold-out methods are unreliable for investment backtests. Published in Journal of Computational Finance (doi 10.21314/jcf.2016.322). [Academic] — [SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4w1110bb/qt4w1110bb.pdf). A free R package `pbo` on CRAN also computes performance degradation and stochastic dominance — [pbo README](https://packages.oit.ncsu.edu/cran/web/packages/pbo/readme/README.html)

**Cross-validation for overlapping financial labels**
- Purged cross-validation removes training observations whose label windows overlap the test set, and an "embargo" adds a buffer after the test set to limit leakage. Combinatorial Purged CV (CPCV), from López de Prado's *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* (2018, Ch. 7 and 12), builds many train/test splits and reassembles them into multiple backtest paths. The result is a distribution of out-of-sample Sharpe ratios rather than the single path that walk-forward gives; for example, N=6 groups with k=2 test groups yields 15 combinations and 5 paths. [Secondary] — [Wikipedia: Purged cross-validation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purged_cross-validation); [fynance docs](https://fynance.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/fynance.data.combinatorial_purged_cv.html); [arXiv 2407.17645 applying CPCV](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.17645). Note: the "15 paths" figure in one skill listing conflates the 15 splits with the 5 paths. By AFML's formula, φ = k/N × C(N,k) = 2/6 × 15 = 5 paths. This is my calculation from the book's formula, not a quoted source.
- Walk-forward produces "a single chronological path that may hinge on one regime sequence." [Secondary] — [Return on Effort](https://returnoneffort.beehiiv.com/p/mitigating-overfitting-advanced-crossvalidation)

**Point-in-time data and delisting**
- Shumway (Journal of Finance 52, 1997) documents that CRSP lacks correct delisting returns for most stocks delisted for negative reasons since 1962, and that the omitted returns are large. The common correction is to impute −30% for performance-related NYSE/Amex delistings with a missing delisting return, and −55% for Nasdaq (Shumway & Warther 1999). After correction, Shumway & Warther find no evidence of a Nasdaq size effect. [Academic, with the imputation figures from a data-provider description] — [IDEAS: Shumway 1997](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v52y1997i1p327-40.html); [IDEAS: Shumway & Warther 1999](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v54y1999i6p2361-2379.html); [SAFE CRSP data description](https://datacenter.safe-frankfurt.de/documents/Data_Description_CRSP_Shumway_20250317.pdf)

**Transaction costs**
- Novy-Marx & Velikov (Review of Financial Studies 29(1):104–147, 2016) find that most anomalies with one-sided monthly turnover below 50% still earn significant net spreads, at least when designed to mitigate costs, and few higher-turnover strategies do. The single most effective simple mitigation is a buy/hold spread: enter a name only at a strict threshold, but keep holding it until it crosses a looser one. Capacity is inversely related to turnover, and size, value and profitability have the greatest capacity. [Academic] — [Novy-Marx PDF](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf); [NBER w20721](https://nber.org/papers/w20721). A secondary rule of thumb: cost drag is roughly 1% of monthly one-sided turnover, so 20% monthly turnover costs about 20 bps a month. [Secondary] — [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2017/05/trading-costs-destroy-factor-investing/)

### Inferences
- **Proposed minimum standard for one person (synthesis, not a single source's prescription):**
  1. **Pre-register and log.** Before testing, write down the hypothesis, the economic rationale, the universe, the horizon and the cost assumptions. Log every variant tried (parameters, universes, holding periods), because the DSR and PBO both need the trial count N. Without the log, a correct DSR cannot be computed.
  2. **Point-in-time data.** Use survivorship-free membership, fundamentals stamped at announcement or filing date rather than fiscal period end, and delisting returns (or a −30%/−55% imputation when missing). Free daily sources such as Yahoo-style feeds are usually survivorship-biased. Treat any backtest on them as an upper bound, or restrict to a universe whose historical membership you can reconstruct.
  3. **Statistical hurdle.** Require a cross-sectional IC t-stat or long-short t-stat of at least 3 for a genuinely new idea. A t of about 2 is acceptable for a replicated, published predictor with economic rationale, given the Chen–Zimmermann evidence. Also require DSR ≥ 0.95 computed with the logged N.
  4. **Out-of-sample evidence.** Hold out the last 20–30% of history, or a separate sub-universe, and touch it once. Additionally run CSCV/PBO across the logged variants and reject if PBO exceeds about 0.5. With small universes and daily data, purge and embargo by at least the label horizon.
  5. **Net of cost.** Use a realistic spread and impact model plus borrow cost for shorts. The signal must survive at the intended turnover, and a buy/hold band is the first thing to try.
  6. **Haircut.** Size on the expectation that live edge will be about 50–65% of the backtest, in line with McLean–Pontiff-style decay.
  7. **Paper trade.** Run a short live paper period (for example one to three months, or at least about 30 independent signal observations) to catch data and timing bugs rather than to prove edge, which takes much longer.
- With small breadth (tens to hundreds of names) and a realistic IC of 0.02–0.05, several years of history are needed before a t-stat of 3 is reachable. That is a strong argument for preferring published, economically motivated signals over novel data-mined ones.

### Gaps
- I did not find a practitioner source that states a formal "minimum standard" for a solo trader. The checklist above is a synthesis.
- The exact McLean–Pontiff published percentages (out-of-sample vs post-publication decay) were not verified from the primary abstract in this session.
- I did not find quantitative guidance on how many walk-forward folds or what holdout length is adequate for daily cross-sectional equity signals.

## Q2. What is the evidence on simple vs optimized signal combinations out of sample?

### Takeaway
Out of sample, estimation error usually swamps the theoretical gains from optimized weights. Equal weighting (1/N) of standardized signals, or of assets, is a hard benchmark to beat, and combination forecasts beat individual predictors mainly because averaging reduces forecast variance. Timing or conditioning factors on regimes or valuations has weak out-of-sample support. The defensible default is to z-score (or rank) each signal cross-sectionally, residualize against known factors, and equal-weight (or risk-weight), with mild shrinkage toward equal weights at most.

### Cited Findings
- DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (Review of Financial Studies 22(5):1915–1953, 2009) evaluate 14 sample-based optimal-portfolio models across seven datasets. None consistently beats 1/N out of sample on Sharpe ratio, certainty-equivalent return or turnover, because estimation error offsets the gains from optimization. For a 25-asset portfolio, the estimation window needed for sample mean-variance to beat 1/N is more than 3,000 months. A secondary summary gives about 6,000 months for 50 assets. [Academic] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i5p1915-1953.html); [LBS](https://london.edu/faculty-and-research/academic-research/o/optimal-versus-naive-diversification-how-inefficient-is-the-1n-portfolio-strategy-p7805); [CEPR DP5142](https://cepr.org/publications/DP5142)
- Rapach, Strauss & Zhou (Review of Financial Studies 23(2):821–862, 2010) find that combining univariate equity-premium forecasts produces statistically and economically significant out-of-sample gains over the historical mean, persisting over time, which individual predictors do not (cf. Welch & Goyal 2008). They attribute the gains to drawing on many variables while reducing forecast volatility. [Academic] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v23y2010i2p821-862.html); [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/out-of-sample-equity-premium-prediction-combination-forecasts-and/). Critique: Löffler (2021) argues the advantages "more or less disappear" against a buy-and-hold benchmark — [Löffler, CFR](https://cfr.ivo-welch.info/published/papers/loeffler2021buyandhold.pdf). These are time-series market-timing results, so they bear only indirectly on cross-sectional stock signals.
- Asness, Chandra, Ilmanen & Israel (AQR; Journal of Portfolio Management 43(5), 2017), "Contrarian Factor Timing is Deceptively Difficult," report that trading simulations of value-spread factor timing "fail to produce economically meaningful improvement in either gross returns or gross Sharpe ratios." They recommend holding a diversified set of "uncorrelated factors that you believe in for the long-term" rather than tactically timing them. They also note that value-based timing largely duplicates the untimed value factor. [Practitioner] — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/contrarian-factor-timing-is-deceptively-difficult); [AQR "Factor Timing is Hard"](https://www.Aqr.Com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Insights/Perspectives/Factor-Timing-is-Hard.pdf). Arnott/Research Affiliates take the opposing view — [Validea summary of the debate](https://blog.validea.com/asness-versus-arnott-the-factor-timing-debate-continues/)
- The regime-conditioning analogue is volatility management: Moreira & Muir's scaling looks strong in-sample, but in Cederburg et al. its real-time version fails in most of 103 strategies because "the scaling parameters shifted" across regimes. Conditioning parameters estimated in-sample are unstable. (Details under Q4.) [Academic] — [Cederburg et al. PDF](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=59058)
- Post-publication, predictor portfolios become more correlated with other published predictors (McLean & Pontiff). This means the effective number of independent signals shrinks over time, which matters for both combination and breadth. [Academic] — [McLean & Pontiff](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/economics/2016-mclean.pdf)

**Measuring signal quality: the fundamental law and its limits**
- Grinold's (1989) Fundamental Law states IR ≈ IC × √Breadth. IC is the correlation between forecasts and realized outcomes, and breadth is the number of independent bets per year. Clarke, de Silva & Thorley (2002, 2006) generalize it to IR = TC × IC × √BR, where the transfer coefficient (0–1) measures how much of the signal survives portfolio constraints such as long-only or position caps. [Secondary summaries of the primary works] — [New Frontier Advisors note](https://newfrontieradvisors.com/media/1744/fundamental-law-september-2019.pdf); [AnalystPrep](https://analystprep.com/study-notes/cfa-level-2/state-and-interpret-the-fundamental-law-of-active-portfolio-management-including-its-component-terms-transfer-coefficient-information-coefficient-breadth-and-active-risk-aggressiveness/); [JOIM paper](https://joim.com/wp-content/uploads/emember/downloads/p0543.pdf)
- Limits: typical ICs are small (about 0.02–0.10). A naive application, such as a monthly IC of 0.04 across 1,050 stocks, implies an annualized IR of about 4.5, which is "implausibly high." The reason is that breadth assumes independent bets, while stocks share factor exposures and signals are autocorrelated over time. [Secondary] — [CFA-style study note](https://pastpaperhero.com/resources/cfa-level2-factor-models-and-active-management-fundamental-law-of-active-management-and-information-ratio); [AnalystPrep](https://analystprep.com/study-notes/?p=19180)

### Inferences
- **Practical combination recipe for a small universe (synthesis):**
  1. For each signal, each day: winsorize (for example at the 1st/99th percentile, or ±3σ), then cross-sectionally z-score or convert to ranks or normal scores. Ranks are more robust with tens of names and fat tails.
  2. Residualize each signal against known exposures (market beta, sector dummies, log size, 12-1 momentum) with a cross-sectional regression, keeping the residual. This makes "new" signals show their incremental information and stops the composite from being a disguised momentum or size bet.
  3. Measure each residualized signal's Spearman rank IC at several horizons (for example 1, 5, 21 and 63 days) to get an IC-decay curve. The horizon where cumulative IC peaks, net of cost, sets the holding period.
  4. Combine with equal weights on the z-scores. If weighting at all, shrink IC- or IR-proportional weights heavily toward equal (for example 50/50 or more), and re-estimate only on expanding windows. Avoid unconstrained regression or mean-variance weights across signals: with a few years of daily cross-sections, the signal covariance and mean IC are estimated far too noisily (DeMiguel et al. logic).
  5. Orthogonalize signals against each other only if two are highly correlated (for example ρ > 0.7). Otherwise equal-weighting already diversifies.
- The fundamental law should be used to set expectations rather than to forecast IR. For tens to a few hundred names, effective breadth after factor correlation is far below the name count, so a realistic composite IR is modest.
- Regime conditioning (for example switching signal weights by VIX level or trend state) multiplies the number of trials and has weak out-of-sample support. Count every regime split as additional trials in the DSR/PBO log. Prefer at most one pre-specified, economically motivated conditioning variable.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a direct study comparing equal-weight vs optimized combinations of cross-sectional stock-selection signals, as distinct from asset weights or time-series forecasts. The evidence above is by analogy. AQR, Robeco and Two Sigma pieces on "mixing vs integrating" composites were not retrieved in this session.
- No primary source was retrieved for IC-decay methodology. It is standard practice (Grinold & Kahn), but uncited here.
- Shrinkage estimators (for example Ledoit–Wolf for covariance) were not separately sourced.

## Q3. How should a proposal record combined evidence (signal scores, confidence, horizon, catalyst date, invalidation) so that its later outcome can attribute performance to each signal? (Includes quantamental scoring and factor-adjusted skill.)

### Takeaway
Record a structured, timestamped snapshot at decision time: each signal's raw value, its z-score or percentile, its factor-residualized value and its weight in the composite; the analyst's discretionary score as a separate numeric "signal"; a probability or confidence; the horizon; the catalyst date; and explicit invalidation conditions. Then evaluate outcomes as factor-adjusted (residual) returns over the stated horizon. That lets you compute each signal's realized IC, including the analyst's own, and judge skill as alpha rather than beta.

### Cited Findings
- Harvey, Rattray, Sinclair & Van Hemert (Man Group/Duke; Journal of Portfolio Management 43(4):55–69, 2017) compare discretionary and systematic hedge funds over 1996–2014. After controlling for volatility and factor exposures, equity-hedge funds have similar net alpha in both groups (about 1.1% vs 1.2% per a secondary summary). Systematic macro funds had higher alpha. The paper discusses "algorithm aversion." The classification is text-based, which critics note may be imprecise. [Practitioner/Academic] — [SSRN 2880641](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2880641); [Duke PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P130_Man_vs_machine.pdf); [CXO summary](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=29357)
- Skill should be measured after factor exposures. The Man vs Machine comparison only became meaningful once volatility and factor exposures were controlled for (same source). Volatility-managed portfolio studies likewise assess value through spanning-regression alphas against the unmanaged factor ([Cederburg et al.](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf)). For free factor returns: Chen–Zimmermann publish monthly long-short returns for about 205 predictors ([Fed](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/open-source-cross-sectional-asset-pricing.htm)).
- Bailey & López de Prado's DSR requires the number of trials and the moments of returns, so the trial log is a required input rather than optional bookkeeping. [Academic] — [SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)

### Inferences
- **Suggested proposal record (one row per proposal, immutable once created; synthesis):**
  - `proposal_id`, `created_at` (timestamp), `ticker`, `direction`, `data_asof` (latest data date used, to keep the record point-in-time)
  - Per signal *i*: `raw_i`, `z_i` (cross-sectional z or percentile), `resid_z_i` (after beta/sector/size/momentum neutralization), `weight_i`, `signal_version_i` (code or parameter hash, linked to the trial log)
  - `composite_score`, plus `expected_excess_return` and `expected_vol` over the horizon
  - `analyst_score` (for example −3..+3, or a probability of outperforming the sector over the horizon) and a short, timestamped `thesis` text, both recorded before seeing the composite where practical, so the two are independent and the analyst's marginal IC can be measured
  - `confidence` as a numeric probability (for example "P(beats sector by >0 over 63d) = 0.6") so outcomes can be scored with a Brier or log score and calibration plotted
  - `horizon_days` (aligned with the IC-decay peak), `catalyst_type` and `catalyst_date` (for example the earnings date), `entry_window`
  - `invalidation`: a price level (for example k × ATR or k × σ from entry), a fundamental condition (for example "guidance cut"), and a time stop (`horizon_days` elapsed)
  - `size_rule` and inputs (target risk, σ estimate, resulting weight)
  - Factor exposures at entry: beta, sector, size and momentum loadings
  - Outcome fields, filled later: realized total return, realized factor-adjusted residual return over the same horizon, max adverse excursion, exit reason, cost paid
- **Attribution:** pool all closed proposals periodically. Compute each signal's realized rank IC between `resid_z_i` and the residual return, and the IC of `analyst_score`. A regression of residual returns on all component scores gives each component's marginal contribution, and it should include the analyst score as one component. If the analyst's marginal IC is near zero after controlling for the quant signals, the judgment is adding noise. Use it as a veto or filter (for example for data errors or pending corporate actions) rather than a weight.
- **Quantamental combination:** treat the analyst score as just another standardized signal with an initially small weight (equal weight at most). Raise it only once a track record of at least about 50–100 independent calls shows positive IC. Keep discretionary overrides logged as trials.
- Because the universe is small and proposals overlap in time, IC estimates from closed proposals will be noisy. Report them with standard errors and, where possible, aggregate by month rather than by trade.

### Gaps
- I found no published, standard schema for "trade proposal" records. The fields above are synthesized from the validation and attribution literature.
- Evidence on calibrating and scoring individual analysts' forecasts (for example Brier scores, or the Tetlock/Good Judgment research) was not retrieved in this session. It should be sourced separately if needed.
- No source was retrieved on the incremental IC of discretionary analysts combined with quant signals ("quantamental") at the stock level. Man vs Machine compares funds, not combined processes.

## Q4. What sizing approach is robust when expected returns are estimated poorly? (Includes timing, holding period and cost budgets.)

### Takeaway
When expected returns are poorly estimated, size on risk rather than on return: give each position roughly equal volatility contribution (inverse-volatility or position-level risk parity), lightly tilted by signal strength and capped, and target overall portfolio volatility. Kelly sizing is very sensitive to errors in the expected-return estimate, so use only a small fraction of it, if any. Volatility targeting has good evidence for reducing tail risk in equities. Moreira–Muir-style variance timing for alpha does not hold up reliably in real time.

### Cited Findings

**Kelly**
- MacLean, Thorp & Ziemba (Quantitative Finance, 2010), "Good and bad properties of the Kelly and fractional Kelly criteria": Kelly asymptotically maximizes growth, but "it is possible, through bad scenarios, to lose most of one's wealth," and it makes very large bets in short-term favorable situations. Fractional Kelly (a fraction f in the Kelly portfolio, 1−f in cash) reduces that risk at the cost of lower expected final wealth. [Academic] — [Berkeley PDF](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf); [IDEAS chapter](https://ideas.repec.org/h/wsi/wschap/9789814293501_0039.html)

**Volatility targeting**
- Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison & Van Hemert (Duke/Man AHL; Journal of Portfolio Management 45(1):14–33, 2018) study 60+ assets with daily data from as early as 1926. Volatility targeting raises Sharpe ratios for "risk assets" (equities, credit), which they link to the leverage effect, but not for bonds, FX or commodities. It consistently reduces the likelihood of extreme returns and the volatility of volatility across all assets, and it cuts maximum drawdowns for 60/40 and risk-parity portfolios. [Practitioner/Academic] — [Duke PDF](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P135_The_impact_of.pdf); [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1370354)
- Moreira & Muir (Journal of Finance 72(4):1611–1644, 2017) scale factor exposure by the inverse of recent variance. They report large alphas and substantially higher Sharpe ratios across factors. A secondary source cites a 4.9% annual alpha for the market strategy. [Academic] — [NBER w22208](https://www.nber.org/papers/22208); [Hedge Fund Alpha summary](https://hedgefundalpha.com/strategies/volatility-managed-portfolios/)
- Cederburg, O'Doherty, Wang & Yan (Journal of Financial Economics 138:95–117, 2020) test 103 equity strategies. The spanning-regression alphas are confirmed, but volatility-managed portfolios "do not systematically outperform their corresponding unmanaged portfolios" in direct real-time comparisons. Out-of-sample versions usually have lower certainty-equivalent returns and Sharpe ratios; CXO reports lower gross Sharpe and CER for 72 of 103. The cause is instability in the spanning-regression parameters used to set the scaling weights. Exceptions include momentum, ROE and BAB. [Academic] — [Lehigh PDF](https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf); [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=32455); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/the-performance-of-volatility-managed-portfolios/). Later rebuttals argue that conditional multifactor versions can beat unconditional ones out of sample net of costs. These are seminar abstracts, not confirmed. [Academic, unreviewed] — [HKU seminar](https://hkubs.hku.hk/event/a-multifactor-perspective-on-volatility-managed-portfolios)

**Estimation error**
- DeMiguel et al.: estimation error in means is the main reason optimized weights lose to 1/N out of sample. This applies directly to sizing from estimated expected returns. [Academic] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i5p1915-1953.html)

**Turnover and holding period**
- Novy-Marx & Velikov: signals with one-sided monthly turnover below about 50% usually survive costs, and buy/hold bands are the most effective mitigation. High-turnover anomalies such as momentum carry much higher costs. [Academic] — [Novy-Marx PDF](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/2017/05/trading-costs-destroy-factor-investing/)

### Inferences
- **Robust default sizing (synthesis):**
  1. Base weight w_i ∝ s_i / σ_i, where s_i is a capped composite score (for example the clipped z-score, or simply ±1 for in/out) and σ_i is an EWMA or 60-day daily volatility.
  2. Normalize so that position-level risk contributions are roughly equal, then scale the whole book to a volatility target (for example 10–15% annualized for a long-short book). Cap any single name at, for example, 5–10% of capital and its risk contribution at, for example, 2× the average. Neutralize or limit net beta and sector exposures.
  3. If using Kelly at all, treat the formula f* = μ/σ² as an upper bound and use ≤ 0.25–0.5 Kelly with a haircut μ: if μ is overestimated by 2×, full Kelly is already over-betting into the region where growth falls. In practice, inverse-volatility sizing with a volatility target is about as good and less fragile.
- **ATR stops vs volatility-scaled sizing:** these are complementary rather than substitutes. Volatility-scaled sizing sets how much is risked per position ex ante. An ATR or σ-multiple stop sets where the thesis is invalidated. Make the stop distance consistent with the signal horizon (for example a stop of k × σ × √horizon), or the stop will exit the trade before a slow signal has time to work. I found no retrieved study showing that ATR stops add value to cross-sectional signals.
- **Timing and catalysts:** align the holding period with the IC-decay peak. Fast signals (days) need a small, liquid universe and tight cost control, while slower fundamental signals (one to three months) tolerate free daily data and modest costs. For earnings catalysts, record the earnings date and decide ex ante whether to hold through it. Event-study style evaluation (abnormal return from day −1 to +1, and drift over the following 60 days) should be measured on factor-adjusted returns.
- **Cost budget:** set a maximum expected cost per year (for example turnover × round-trip cost ≤ 1/3 of expected gross alpha), apply buy/hold bands, and include borrow costs for shorts. Hard-to-borrow small caps can carry fees large enough to eliminate short-side alpha.
- Volatility targeting at the portfolio level is defensible, because it reduces tail risk and is especially helpful for equities. Volatility timing as an alpha source, with scaling fitted on past regressions, is not reliably robust in real time and should be treated as another tested trial.
